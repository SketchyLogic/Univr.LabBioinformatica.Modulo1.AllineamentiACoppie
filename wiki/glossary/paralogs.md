# paralogs

**Paralogs** are **[[homology|homologous]] sequences *within a single species* that arose from *gene duplication*.** [[raw/Teoria_L3_ALLINEAMENTI_A_COPPIE_25-26.pdf#page=4|L3 p.4]]

The defining event is **gene duplication**: an ancestral gene is copied within the *same* genome, leaving two (or more) sister genes. Freed from the pressure to keep doing the original job, the copies can diverge and often **acquire new but related functions**.

The classic example is the **human globin family**: α-globin, β-globin, δ-globin, the γ-globins, ε-globin, ζ-globin, myoglobin, cytoglobin, neuroglobin… all paralogs descended by repeated duplication of an early globin gene, now specialised (e.g. hemoglobin chains for blood-oxygen transport, myoglobin for muscle oxygen storage). [[raw/Teoria_L3_ALLINEAMENTI_A_COPPIE_25-26.pdf#page=4|human globin paralog tree, L3 p.4]]

Contrast with [[orthologs]] (different species, via speciation); the full comparison is in [[orthologs vs paralogs]]. Both are homology — only the separating **event** differs.

> [!Hint] Mnemonic
> **Para**logs grow in **Pa**rallel inside one genome (duplication). **Ortho**logs sit in **O**ther organisms (speciation).

# TLDR
Paralogs are **homologous genes *within a single species*** created by **gene duplication**. With one copy covering the original job, the other is free to diverge and often acquires a new but related function.

# Read aloud
KP.0.Definition: What paralogs are.
Paralogs are homologous sequences within a single species that arose from gene duplication.

KP.1.Concept: The defining event is duplication.
The key event is gene duplication. An ancestral gene gets copied inside the same genome, leaving two sister genes side by side. Because one copy can keep doing the original job, the other is free to drift and often picks up a new, related function.

KP.2.Concept: The human globin family.
The classic example is the human globin family. Alpha-globin, beta-globin, delta, the gamma globins, myoglobin, cytoglobin, neuroglobin, and more — all of them are paralogs, born from repeated duplications of one early globin gene, and now specialised for different tasks. The hemoglobin chains carry oxygen in the blood, while myoglobin stores it in muscle.

KP.3.Connection: Contrast with orthologs.
Paralogs are the mirror image of orthologs. Orthologs live in different species and are separated by speciation; paralogs live in the same species and are separated by duplication. Both are homologs — only the separating event differs.

KP.4.CoolFact: Genome duplications mass-produced paralogs.
And here's the cool part — entire genomes have been duplicated wholesale. Two rounds of genome duplication early in vertebrate evolution are thought to be why we vertebrates have whole gene families where simpler animals have just one gene. It was a major engine of our complexity.

# Question and Answer
Q. Define paralogs.
A. Homologous sequences within a single species that arose from gene duplication.

Q. What event creates paralogs?
A. Gene duplication — copying a gene within the same genome.

Q. Why can paralogs evolve new functions?
A. One copy preserves the original job, freeing the other to diverge and specialise.

Q. Give the textbook example of paralogs.
A. The human globin family (α, β, δ, γ, ε, ζ globins, myoglobin, cytoglobin, neuroglobin), all from duplications of an early globin gene.

Q. How do paralogs differ from orthologs?
A. Paralogs: same species, gene duplication. Orthologs: different species, speciation. See [[orthologs vs paralogs]].

Q. What large-scale event can produce many paralogs at once?
A. Whole-genome duplication (e.g. the vertebrate 2R rounds).
